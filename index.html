Pets and Animal Welfare in Australia

Understanding how Australians care for animals

Section 1: Pet Ownership

Pet ownership plays an important role in Australian society. This section explores how pet ownership has changed over the past decade and which types of pets are most popular among Australian households.

According to Animal Medicines Australia, the proportion of Australian households owning at least one pet increased from 62% in 2016 to 73% in 2025, representing an increase of 11 percentage points. This suggests that pet ownership has become increasingly common among Australian households.

Source: Animal Medicines Australia, Pets in Australia 2025, page 6.

Dogs are the most commonly owned pets in Australia, with 49.3% of households owning at least one dog in 2025, followed by cats at 33.6%. Although fish are owned by a smaller proportion of households, they account for the largest estimated number of individual pets.

Source: Animal Medicines Australia, Pets in Australia 2025, page 7.

Section 2: Veterinary Care & Responsibility

Owning a pet involves more than companionship. Australian households also spend money on food, veterinary services and other aspects of pet care. These expenses vary across different pet types.

In 2025, Australian households with dogs reported the highest average annual spending among the surveyed pet types, at A$2,520 per household. Households with cats spent an average of A$1,656. Reptile-owning households reported the highest average veterinary service spending at A$692, although the reptile-owner survey sample was relatively small. These figures represent average expenditure per household, rather than per individual animal.

Source: Animal Medicines Australia, Pets in Australia 2025, page 42 (PDF page 45). Note: Other costs are calculated by subtracting food and veterinary service expenses from the reported total annual household expenditure.

Vaccination is an important part of preventative pet healthcare. According to the 2025 survey, 96% of dogs and 90% of cats had been vaccinated at least once. However, the proportions vaccinated within the past 12 months were lower, at 77% for dogs and 66% for cats. These figures describe different time periods and do not necessarily indicate whether pets are overdue for vaccination.

Source: Animal Medicines Australia, Pets in Australia 2025, page 59 (PDF page 62).

Section 3: Animal Shelters & Adoption

Animal shelters play an important role in caring for animals that have been surrendered, rescued or otherwise brought into care. RSPCA Australia publishes annual statistics on the animals received by its participating societies. This section examines how reported animal numbers have changed and what happens to animals in RSPCA care.

RSPCA statistics show that the total number of animals received decreased from 103,057 in 2020–21 to 77,464 in 2024–25, a reduction of approximately 24.8%. The largest relative decline was observed among cats, at 39.8%, followed by dogs at 28.9%. In comparison, the number of other animals decreased by approximately 5.8%.

These figures describe animals received by RSPCA societies, not all animals in Australia. Changes in reported numbers do not necessarily indicate an improvement or deterioration in overall animal welfare.

Source: RSPCA Australia, National Statistics 2024–2025, Table 2, PDF page 5. Note: Percentages are calculated relative to the 2020–21 financial year. Other animals includes animal categories other than dogs and cats.

The 2024–25 RSPCA data reveal different outcomes for dogs and cats. Of the 15,852 dogs received, 6,839 (43.1%) were rehomed, compared with 16,445 (64.1%) of the 25,639 cats. Dogs were more likely to be reclaimed by their owners: 3,203 dogs (20.2%) versus 979 cats (3.8%). The 2024–25 RSPCA data show differences in the numbers of dogs and cats received across Australian states and territories. Seven of the eight states and territories reported receiving more cats than dogs. Western Australia was the only exception, receiving 975 dogs and 920 cats. Queensland reported the highest numbers of both dogs and cats, with 6,401 dogs and 8,019 cats received.

Section 4: Geographic Differences

Animal shelter outcomes vary across Australian states and territories. This section explores geographic differences in animal care using data reported by participating RSPCA societies. The first map compares the proportion of dogs and cats rehomed relative to the number received during the 2024–25 financial year.

The Australian Capital Territory (ACT) had the highest reported rehomed-to-received ratio among the eight states and territories, at approximately 68.9%. In comparison, Western Australia (WA) had the lowest ratio, at approximately 50.2%. These differences highlight variation in reported RSPCA shelter outcomes across Australian regions.

These figures represent animals received and rehomed by participating RSPCA societies. They should not be interpreted as statewide adoption rates or direct measures of animal welfare, since reporting coverage and intake responsibilities differ between societies.

Source: RSPCA Australia, National Statistics 2024–2025, Table 4, PDF pages 7–8. Note: Rehoming ratios are calculated by dividing the combined number of dogs and cats rehomed by the combined number of dogs and cats received within each state or territory. Geographic boundaries: Australian States GeoJSON, rowanhogan/australian-states (GitHub).

The geographic distribution of animals received by RSPCA societies also varies considerably. Queensland reported the largest number of animals received in 2024–25, with 43,041 animals, followed by the Northern Territory with 12,410. Queensland's total included 26,564 wildlife animals, highlighting the importance of wildlife care within its reported intake.

The circles are positioned approximately within each state or territory, with their areas proportional to the number of animals received. These values reflect RSPCA reporting and service coverage, rather than the total number of animals needing care in each state. Larger circles do not necessarily indicate poorer animal welfare.

Source: RSPCA Australia, National Statistics 2024–2025, Table 4, PDF pages 7–8. Note: Total animals received includes dogs, cats, horses, livestock, wildlife and other animals. Circle positions are representative locations within each state or territory, not the locations of individual RSPCA facilities. Geographic boundaries: Australian States GeoJSON, rowanhogan/australian-states (GitHub).

The share of wildlife among animals received by participating RSPCA societies differs across states and territories. Queensland reports a particularly high wildlife share, with 26,564 wildlife animals out of 43,041 animals received in 2024–25, or approximately 61.7%.

Each line and dot represents the wildlife share in one state or territory. States are ranked by percentage, and Queensland is highlighted for comparison.

Source: RSPCA Australia, National Statistics 2024–2025, Table 4, PDF pages 7–8. Note: Wildlife share is calculated as wildlife received divided by total animals received for each state or territory. These figures describe participating RSPCA societies, not all wildlife rescue activity in Australia.

The number of dogs and cats received by RSPCA societies differs considerably across Australian states and territories. In Queensland, 8,019 cats and 6,401 dogs were received during 2024–25, while the Northern Territory received 6,621 cats and 3,320 dogs.
